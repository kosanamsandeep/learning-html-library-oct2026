Unsupervised ML

Clustering algorithms explained

Clustering groups data into meaningful segments without predefined labels. It is one of the core unsupervised learning techniques. Interviews test whether you understand the algorithm mechanics, what each one assumes about the data, and how to evaluate clusters without ground truth.

Core idea

Given unlabeled data X, find groups where points within a group are more similar than points across groups

Why clustering matters

Customer segmentation, anomaly detection, topic discovery, document grouping, and data exploration all rely on clustering. It is everywhere in applied data science.

The hard part

There is no label to optimize directly. You must define what "similar" means, choose the right algorithm, and evaluate quality with indirect metrics.

Algorithm 1

K-Means

How it works

  1. Choose K initial cluster centers randomly or with K-Means++.
  2. Assign each point to the nearest center.
  3. Recompute centers as the mean of assigned points.
  4. Repeat steps 2 and 3 until convergence.

Key properties

  • Simple, fast, and scales well.
  • Assumes clusters are roughly spherical and equal-sized.
  • You must specify K in advance.
  • Sensitive to initialization and outliers.

How to pick K: use the elbow method (plot inertia vs K and look for the bend) or the silhouette score. Neither is perfect, but together they give a reasonable starting point.

Algorithm 2

DBSCAN

How it works

DBSCAN groups points that are closely packed together based on a distance threshold (epsilon) and a minimum number of neighbors (minPts). Points that cannot be reached are labeled as noise.

Key properties

  • Finds arbitrarily shaped clusters, not just spheres.
  • Identifies outliers naturally as noise points.
  • Does not require specifying number of clusters in advance.
  • Sensitive to epsilon and minPts. Struggles with varying density.

Algorithm 3

Hierarchical clustering

How it works

Agglomerative clustering starts with each point as its own cluster and repeatedly merges the two closest clusters. The result is a tree called a dendrogram. You cut the tree at a chosen level to get K clusters.

Key properties

  • Produces a full hierarchy of cluster relationships.
  • No need to specify K upfront; you choose where to cut.
  • Different linkage methods give different results: single, complete, average, Ward.
  • Does not scale well to very large datasets.
  • Can be sensitive to outliers because unusual points distort merge distances.

1. Start with every point as its own cluster

If you have 100 points, you begin with 100 tiny clusters. At this stage, nothing has been grouped yet.

2. Merge the two closest clusters

The algorithm repeatedly finds the nearest pair of current clusters and merges them. The definition of "nearest" depends on the linkage rule you choose.

3. Record every merge in a tree

This merge history becomes the dendrogram. Unlike K-Means, hierarchical clustering gives you not just one final answer, but the full history of how clusters formed.

4. Cut the tree where you want the final granularity

You draw a horizontal line across the dendrogram. That line is the chosen level. Where the line cuts branches determines the final clusters.

Dendrogram intuition

distance ↑
|
|            ┌───── A
|        ┌───┤
|        |   └───── B
|    ┌───┤
|    |   └────────── C
|────┤
|    └────────────── D
|
+------------------------------> data points

Each merge happens at a certain height.
That height represents how far apart the clusters were when merged.

What does “chosen level” really mean?

The chosen level is the height at which you cut the dendrogram. That height is a distance threshold. Below that line, clusters are similar enough to stay merged. Above that line, they are considered too different and stay separate.

How this differs from K-Means

K-Means asks you to choose K upfront. Hierarchical clustering first builds the whole tree, then lets you decide the final number of clusters by choosing the cut height afterward.

Cut height What happens Result
Low cut You stop merging early Many small clusters
Middle cut You allow moderate merging Moderate number of clusters
High cut You allow large-distance merges Few large clusters, eventually one cluster

One-line definition: the chosen level is the distance or height at which you cut the dendrogram to decide the final number of clusters.

How people choose this level in practice

  • Look for a large vertical jump in the dendrogram and cut before that jump.
  • Use a business rule, such as a maximum acceptable dissimilarity.
  • Choose the granularity you want: macro segments or micro segments.

Why outliers are a problem

An outlier often stays alone until very late and only merges at a large distance. That can stretch the dendrogram scale and make the cut harder to interpret. Depending on linkage, unusual points can also distort which clusters appear close to each other.

Single linkage is especially sensitive because it can create chaining effects. Complete, average, or Ward linkage are often more stable, but you should still inspect outliers before trusting the hierarchy.

Algorithm 4

Gaussian mixture models

How it works

GMM assumes data is generated from a mixture of K Gaussian distributions. It uses the EM algorithm to estimate the mean, covariance, and mixing weight of each component.

Key properties

  • Produces soft assignments with probabilities per cluster.
  • Handles elliptical cluster shapes better than K-Means.
  • More flexible but needs more data and is slower.
  • Model selection: use BIC or AIC to choose the number of components.

Quality measurement

How to evaluate clusters

Metric What it measures When to use
Silhouette score How similar a point is to its own cluster vs the nearest other cluster. General-purpose cluster quality without labels. Range -1 to 1.
Inertia (within-cluster sum of squares) Total squared distance from each point to its cluster center. Elbow method for K-Means. Lower is tighter.
Davies-Bouldin index Average similarity ratio of each cluster with the most similar cluster. Lower is better. Alternative to silhouette for large data.
Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) Agreement between predicted clusters and true labels, adjusted for chance. Only usable when ground-truth labels exist. Range -1 to 1.
Situation Good first choice Why
Roughly spherical, equal-sized clusters K-Means Fast and simple. Most common baseline.
Arbitrary shapes, outliers present DBSCAN Handles noise and non-spherical clusters.
Want a hierarchy of groupings Agglomerative clustering Dendrogram gives structure at multiple levels.
Want soft probabilistic assignments GMM Points can belong to multiple clusters with probabilities.

Interview readiness

Questionnaire and scenarios

Question

How does K-Means differ from DBSCAN?

Strong answer

K-Means needs K specified upfront, assumes spherical clusters, and assigns every point. DBSCAN finds clusters of arbitrary shape, detects noise, and does not require K. K-Means is simpler and faster; DBSCAN handles messier real-world data better.

Question

What is the silhouette score?

Strong answer

It measures how similar a point is to its own cluster compared to the nearest other cluster. A score near 1 means good cluster fit, near 0 means the point is on the boundary, and negative means it may be in the wrong cluster.

Question

What does it mean to cut a dendrogram at a chosen level?

Strong answer

It means choosing a height or distance threshold on the dendrogram. Wherever that horizontal cut intersects the tree determines the final clusters. Lower cuts give more clusters, higher cuts give fewer.

Scenario

You are segmenting customers but some customers do not fit any clear group. Which algorithm handles this better?

Expected reasoning

DBSCAN, because it naturally labels outlier points as noise instead of forcing them into a cluster. K-Means would force every customer into a group.

Scenario

Your dendrogram has one point merging only at a very large height. What should you suspect?

Expected reasoning

I would suspect an outlier or very unusual observation. That point is far from the rest, so it stays alone until late in the merge process. I would inspect it before trusting the final cut level.

Scenario

Your K-Means clustering keeps giving different results every run. What is happening?

Expected reasoning

K-Means is sensitive to random initialization. I would use K-Means++ initialization, run multiple times with different seeds, or increase n_init to pick the best result automatically.
