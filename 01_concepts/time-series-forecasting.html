Forecasting and sequence data

Time series forecasting: ARIMA, SARIMA, Holt-Winters, and deep learning

Time series forecasting predicts future values using observations ordered in time. The key point is that time order matters. Yesterday influences today, seasonality repeats, trends drift, shocks happen, and mistakes in validation can easily leak future information into the model.

Interviews usually test whether you understand when to use classical models like ARIMA or Holt-Winters, when deep learning is justified, what metrics actually make sense, and what to do when the history is short, noisy, or irregular.

Core forecasting idea

future value = trend + seasonality + autoregressive memory + shocks + noise

What makes time series different

Rows are not exchangeable. In ordinary regression, you can often shuffle data safely. In time series, shuffling breaks the problem because past and future are fundamentally different.

Common business examples

Daily sales, weekly traffic, hourly demand, energy load, call center volume, inventory consumption, stock movement, and sensor measurements are all time series problems.

Building blocks

Trend, seasonality, cycles, and noise

Component Meaning Example
Trend Long-term increase or decrease Sales slowly rising month over month
Seasonality Pattern repeating at fixed intervals Weekend spikes, holiday dips, yearly festival demand
Cycles Longer repeating movement, not perfectly regular Economic expansion and slowdown
Noise Unexplained random variation Weather shock, one-off outage, unexpected promotion effect

Additive vs multiplicative: if seasonal swings stay about the same size over time, think additive. If seasonal swings grow as the series level grows, think multiplicative. This is a practical clue for transformations and Holt-Winters settings.

Practical flow

How forecasting projects are usually done

1. Inspect the series

Check frequency, missing timestamps, outliers, trend, seasonality, regime changes, and whether the business process has special events like promotions or holidays.

2. Make it model-ready

Resample, impute missing timestamps, create calendar features, consider log transforms, differencing, or deseasonalization depending on the model family.

3. Choose a baseline first

Always compare against naive baselines like last value, seasonal naive, or simple moving average. Many fancy models look good only because the baseline was weak.

4. Validate chronologically

Use train-then-validate on future periods, or rolling-origin backtesting. Never randomly shuffle time series data for evaluation.

Classical model 1

ARIMA

What ARIMA means

ARIMA stands for AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average. It combines three ideas:

  • AR(p): use previous values to predict the next value.
  • I(d): difference the series to make it more stationary.
  • MA(q): use previous forecast errors to improve the next forecast.

ARIMA(p, d, q)

When it works well

  • Univariate series with clear autocorrelation structure.
  • History is not too short.
  • Series is reasonably stable after differencing.
  • You want an interpretable classical baseline.

Stationarity matters: ARIMA prefers a stable statistical structure. If the mean, variance, or autocorrelation changes wildly over time, raw ARIMA often struggles unless you transform the series first.

Classical model 2

SARIMA

What SARIMA adds

SARIMA extends ARIMA by adding seasonal autoregressive, seasonal differencing, and seasonal moving average terms.

SARIMA(p, d, q)(P, D, Q, s)

When to use it

Use SARIMA when you have repeating patterns at fixed intervals, such as day-of-week, month, quarter, or year. If a series has strong seasonality, SARIMA is often a much better baseline than plain ARIMA.

Important limitation

SARIMA still assumes a fairly regular repeating seasonal pattern. If seasonality changes because of promotions, holidays, or sudden operational changes, you may need exogenous variables or a more flexible model.

Classical model 3

Holt-Winters exponential smoothing

What it models

Holt-Winters explicitly models level, trend, and seasonality using exponentially weighted updates. Recent observations matter more than distant ones.

Why people use it

  • Very strong baseline for business time series.
  • Works well when trend and seasonality are stable.
  • Simpler and often faster to set up than ARIMA-family tuning.
  • Useful when you want an interpretable decomposition.
Version Use when
Simple exponential smoothing No trend, no seasonality
Holt Trend but no seasonality
Holt-Winters additive Seasonality is roughly constant in size
Holt-Winters multiplicative Seasonality scales with the series level

Other useful baselines

Other classical forecasting approaches

Seasonal naive and moving average

These simple baselines are essential. Seasonal naive says “next Monday looks like last Monday.” If your fancy model cannot beat seasonal naive, it is not production-ready.

Prophet and regression-style models

Prophet-like approaches explicitly model trend, seasonality, holidays, and changepoints. Regression models with lag features, rolling means, and calendar features are also common and often strong in business forecasting pipelines.

Modern approaches

Deep learning frameworks and model families

Model family When it is used Main tradeoff
LSTM / GRU Sequential forecasting with moderate history and nonlinear temporal effects Can work well, but may be harder to tune and slower than simpler baselines
Temporal CNN / TCN Longer memory with convolutional sequence modeling Often more stable than RNNs, but still data-hungry
Transformer-style forecasting Large multivariate forecasting, long context, many related series Powerful but expensive and needs good scale
N-BEATS / N-HiTS / TFT Advanced forecasting benchmarks and large business forecasting problems Strong performance, but more engineering and tuning complexity

When deep learning is justified

  • You have many related time series, not just one series.
  • You have long history and enough data volume.
  • You need to combine time signals with exogenous features, holidays, prices, promotions, or static metadata.
  • You care about complex nonlinear interactions and have the engineering capacity to support them.

When deep learning is overkill

  • History is short.
  • The pattern is mostly trend plus seasonality.
  • You only have one or a few series.
  • A seasonal naive, Holt-Winters, or SARIMA baseline already performs strongly.

Practical tooling: teams commonly use statsmodels for ARIMA-family models, Prophet for decomposable trend-seasonality forecasting, and PyTorch Forecasting, Darts, GluonTS, NeuralForecast, or custom PyTorch or TensorFlow code for deep learning workflows.

Evaluation

Which forecasting metrics matter?

Metric What it measures Important caveat
MAE Average absolute forecast error Easy to explain, but scale-dependent
RMSE Squares errors before averaging Punishes large misses more heavily than MAE
MAPE Average percentage error Can explode or become meaningless when actual values are zero or near zero
sMAPE Symmetric percentage-style error More stable than MAPE, but still not perfect
WAPE Total absolute error divided by total actual value Useful for portfolio-level business reporting
MASE Error scaled by naive forecast error Very useful because it compares your model against a naive baseline

Do we use MAPE?

Yes, many business teams ask for MAPE because percentage error is intuitive. But you should not use it blindly. If actual values can be zero, very small, or highly intermittent, MAPE becomes unstable and can mislead stakeholders.

Better metric choices by situation

Use MAE or RMSE when scale matters, MASE when you want to compare against a naive baseline, and WAPE for aggregate business reporting. If stockouts, sparse demand, or zeros are common, prefer MASE, WAPE, or MAE over raw MAPE.

Data volume matters

Low history vs large history datasets

Situation What usually works better Main concern
Low history, one series Naive, Holt-Winters, simple ARIMA, regression with calendar features Overfitting and false confidence
Moderate history, clear seasonality SARIMA, Holt-Winters, feature-based gradient boosting Modeling seasonality and validation correctly
Large history, many related series Global models, boosted feature pipelines, deep learning models Scalability, feature engineering, and distribution shift

Low history datasets

  • Prefer simpler models with stronger inductive bias.
  • Do not expect deep learning to rescue tiny datasets.
  • Use domain features like holidays, promotions, and calendar effects.
  • Check whether a seasonal naive baseline is already hard to beat.

Large history datasets

  • Global models can learn across many related series.
  • Deep models become more justifiable.
  • Feature pipelines and multivariate inputs often help more than univariate-only models.
  • Computation, retraining strategy, and drift monitoring become important.

Data preparation

Important transformations and preprocessing steps

Resampling and frequency alignment

Make sure the time index is regular: hourly means hourly, daily means daily. Irregular timestamps often break assumptions and confuse models.

Missing timestamp handling

Distinguish between truly missing observations and real zero demand. Treating these as the same thing can destroy metric interpretation and model quality.

Log transform or Box-Cox

Useful when variance grows with the level of the series. These transforms stabilize variance and make multiplicative behavior easier to model.

Differencing

Subtract the previous value or previous seasonal value to remove trend or seasonality. This is central to ARIMA-family modeling.

Lag and rolling-window features

Create features like previous day, previous week, rolling mean, rolling std, and moving max. These are powerful in gradient-boosted or regression-based forecasting pipelines.

Calendar and event features

Day of week, month, quarter, holiday flags, promotions, stockouts, and price changes often matter more than the raw model choice.

Outlier treatment

Forecasting models are very sensitive to shocks. Keep meaningful business spikes if they are real, but flag data errors separately. Blindly smoothing everything can remove real demand signals.

Critical rule

Validation rules you must not break

Use walk-forward or rolling-origin validation

Train on the past, validate on the future, then roll the window forward. This mirrors real forecasting use and prevents future leakage.

Never randomly shuffle time series rows

Random train-test splitting leaks future information into training. A model may look amazing offline and fail immediately in production if you validate incorrectly.

Interview-ready summary

Start with naive and seasonal baselines, inspect trend and seasonality, use ARIMA-family or Holt-Winters for structured classical patterns, move to deep learning only when history and scale justify it, and choose metrics carefully because MAPE can be misleading on zero-heavy data.

Interview readiness

Questionnaire and scenarios

Question

What is the difference between ARIMA and SARIMA?

Strong answer

ARIMA models non-seasonal autoregressive, differencing, and moving-average behavior. SARIMA adds seasonal autoregressive, seasonal differencing, and seasonal moving-average terms for repeating patterns like weekly or yearly seasonality.

Question

Should we use MAPE for forecasting?

Strong answer

You can use MAPE when actual values are safely away from zero and percentage error is important to the business. But if zeros or near-zeros are common, MAPE becomes unstable, so I would prefer MASE, WAPE, MAE, or RMSE depending on the business objective.

Question

When is deep learning justified for forecasting?

Strong answer

Usually when you have large history, many related series, rich exogenous signals, and a business need that simpler baselines cannot meet. For a small single-series problem, classical methods are often more appropriate.

Scenario

You have only 18 months of monthly sales history with clear seasonality. What would you try first?

Expected reasoning

I would start with seasonal naive, Holt-Winters, and possibly SARIMA if the seasonal structure is stable. Deep learning would be hard to justify with such limited history.

Scenario

You forecast thousands of related SKUs with promotions, holidays, and price changes. What changes?

Expected reasoning

Now global models become attractive. I would consider feature-based gradient boosting or deep forecasting models that can learn across many series while using exogenous variables. I would still keep strong seasonal naive and classical baselines for comparison.

Scenario

Your forecast looks excellent offline, but fails in production immediately. What is the first thing you suspect?

Expected reasoning

I would first suspect leakage from incorrect validation, especially random train-test split on time-ordered data. The second suspicion would be a regime change or exogenous factor mismatch between training and production.
