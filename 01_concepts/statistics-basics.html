Math and statistics foundations

Descriptive vs inferential statistics

Statistics is the backbone of data science. Descriptive statistics summarizes what you already have. Inferential statistics uses samples to make claims about a larger population.

Interviews test whether you know when to describe data versus when to draw conclusions from it, and whether you understand the assumptions behind both.

Core distinction

Descriptive = summarize the data you have Inferential = generalize from a sample to a population

Why statistics appears in AI interviews

Every model evaluation, A/B test, feature analysis, and data quality check depends on statistical thinking. Interviewers want to confirm that you are not just applying formulas blindly.

The two branches

Descriptive statistics gives you the shape of the data. Inferential statistics tells you whether the patterns you see are real or likely due to random variation.

Branch 1

Descriptive statistics

Measure What it tells you When to use it
Mean The arithmetic average of values. Useful for roughly symmetric distributions without extreme outliers.
Median The middle value when sorted. Better for skewed distributions or when outliers exist.
Mode The most frequent value. Useful for categorical data or discrete distributions.
Standard deviation How spread out values are from the mean. Primary measure of variability.
Variance The square of standard deviation. Used in many formulas and statistical tests internally.
Percentiles and IQR Where data points fall relative to the distribution. Great for understanding spread and detecting outliers.
Skewness Direction and degree of asymmetry in the distribution. Tells you if the tail is heavier on the left or right.
Kurtosis How heavy the tails are relative to a normal distribution. Useful for understanding extreme-value risk.

Practical rule: always look at median and IQR alongside mean and standard deviation. If they disagree significantly, you probably have skew or outliers.

Branch 2

Inferential statistics

What inference means

You observe a sample and want to say something about the population it came from. The sample mean is your best estimate, but it carries uncertainty. Inferential statistics quantifies that uncertainty.

Core tools

  • Confidence intervals: range where the true parameter likely falls.
  • Hypothesis tests: formal way to test a claim about the population.
  • P-values: probability of observing data this extreme if the null hypothesis is true.
  • Significance level: your pre-set threshold for rejecting the null.
Aspect Descriptive Inferential
Goal Summarize and visualize existing data. Draw conclusions about a population from a sample.
Uncertainty No uncertainty statements. You report what you see. Explicitly quantifies uncertainty with intervals or tests.
Typical output Mean, median, standard deviation, histograms, box plots. P-values, confidence intervals, test statistics.
Assumptions Minimal. You are describing what is there. Requires assumptions about sampling, distribution, independence.
Example Average salary of surveyed employees is 85000. The true population average salary is between 82000 and 88000 with 95% confidence.

Modeling intuition

Why distributions matter at all

A distribution is a shape for uncertainty

A distribution is the mathematical description of what values are possible, how likely they are, and how variable they can be. It is the bridge between raw data and statistical decisions.

Why it is required in practice

Different data types behave differently. Counts, waiting times, binary events, heavy-tailed sales, and sampled averages do not follow the same rules. If you force the wrong distributional assumption, your intervals, tests, forecasts, and business conclusions can be materially wrong.

1. The data type decides the starting shortlist

Binary outcomes suggest Bernoulli or Binomial. Non-negative counts suggest Poisson-like models. Positive right-skewed magnitudes suggest log-normal, gamma, or Pareto-like behavior. Sample means often lead back to the normal or t-distribution.

2. The distribution controls the tools you can justify

Confidence intervals, p-values, variance tests, and likelihood-based models are derived from assumptions about distributional shape. That is why interviews ask not just for a formula, but for the assumptions behind it.

3. The wrong distribution creates the wrong business summary

When outlet sales are highly skewed, the mean overstates the “typical” outlet. When counts have more variability than Poisson allows, alerts and confidence bands become too narrow. Distribution choice is not theory for theory’s sake; it changes decisions.

Must know

Key distributions and when to apply them

Mental model: the normal distribution is important, but it is not the default shape of raw business data. Raw data is often skewed, zero-heavy, count-based, or heavy-tailed. Normality often appears only after aggregation, averaging, or transformation.

Distribution Data pattern When to use it Why it exists
Bernoulli One yes/no trial, outcome is 0 or 1. Single conversion, visit happened or not, fraud flag, defect present or not. It is the simplest distribution for binary uncertainty.
Binomial Number of successes in a fixed number of trials. Orders placed out of 10 calls, successful deliveries out of 40 attempts. It extends Bernoulli to repeated fixed-count experiments.
Poisson Event counts over time, area, or space. Walk-in customers per hour, defects per batch, incidents per day. It models independent counts when events are relatively rare and the mean roughly matches the variance.
Negative Binomial Counts with extra variability compared to Poisson. Daily complaints, support tickets, or missed deliveries when variance is much larger than the mean. It exists because real count data is often more dispersed than Poisson allows.
Uniform All values in a range are equally likely. Random number generation, unbiased random assignment, lottery-style selection. It is the neutral distribution when there is no preference for any value in the interval.
Exponential Positive waiting time until the next event. Time to next customer arrival, time until machine failure, inter-arrival time. It models memoryless waiting time: the chance of waiting longer does not depend on how long you have already waited.
Gamma Positive continuous quantity with right skew. Total service time, insurance claim size, sum of several waiting times. It generalizes exponential behavior to more flexible positive skew.
Log-Normal Positive data driven by multiplicative growth. Income, outlet sales, transaction size, delivery times with right skew. It exists because many business processes compound multiplicatively rather than add linearly.
Pareto Heavy-tailed positive data where a few observations dominate. Top outlets generating most revenue, wealth, viral content reach. It captures “80/20” behavior and extreme inequality better than normal-like models.
Cauchy Extreme-tailed behavior with unstable averages. Mostly used as a warning case in modeling and inference. It shows what breaks when you assume the mean and variance always behave nicely; for Cauchy, they do not.
Normal (Gaussian) Symmetric continuous data or aggregated averages. Measurement noise, average process output, residuals under many classical models. It matters because the central limit theorem makes averages and many estimators approximately normal.
t-distribution Small-sample inference for means with unknown variance. Confidence interval or test for a sample mean with limited data. It keeps extra uncertainty in the tails when sample size is small.
Chi-square Positive distribution built from squared normal variables. Variance estimation, contingency-table tests, goodness-of-fit tests. It appears naturally when measuring how far observed values deviate from expected values in squared units.
F-distribution Ratio of two scaled variances. ANOVA, comparing model fits, testing whether groups have different means. It exists because many “compare explained variance vs unexplained variance” tests reduce to a variance ratio.
Mixture distributions Data with multiple sub-populations or multiple peaks. Urban vs rural outlet sales, budget vs premium customers, low and high performers. They exist because one single distribution is often too simple for heterogeneous real populations.

Retail and sales intuition

Raw outlet sales are usually right-skewed, often with heavy tails. A small number of outlets contribute a large share of revenue. That makes log-normal and Pareto-like thinking more realistic than forcing a symmetric normal model.

Why the mean can mislead

If one mega-outlet sells 20 times more than a typical store, the average outlet sales number stops describing a “typical” outlet. In that situation median, percentiles, and tail-aware models are usually more honest summaries.

Practical diagnosis

How to recognize which distribution family fits

1. Start with the support of the data

Ask what values are even possible. Only 0 or 1 suggests Bernoulli. Non-negative integers suggest count distributions like Poisson or Negative Binomial. Positive continuous values suggest exponential, gamma, log-normal, or Pareto-like models.

2. Look at shape, skew, and tails

Symmetric bell-shaped data may justify normal assumptions. Strong right skew points toward log-normal or gamma. Very heavy tails with a few dominant observations suggest Pareto-like behavior. Multiple peaks suggest a mixture rather than one clean distribution.

3. Compare mean and variance for counts

If a count variable has variance close to the mean, Poisson may be reasonable. If the variance is much larger than the mean, Negative Binomial is usually a better candidate. If there are too many zeros, you may need a zero-inflated model.

4. Think about the data-generating mechanism

Waiting until the next arrival suggests exponential. Summing multiple waiting stages suggests gamma. Multiplicative growth suggests log-normal. Repeated independent yes/no trials suggest binomial. Distribution choice is strongest when the math matches the business mechanism.

If the data looks like this Candidate distribution Reason

Successes out of a fixed number of attempts Binomial Repeated yes/no trials with fixed trial count.
0, 1, 2, 3... event counts over time Poisson Classic count process with mean close to variance.

Practical workflow: start with a histogram or box plot, check whether values are binary, counts, or continuous, compare mean vs median, compare mean vs variance for counts, and ask whether the business mechanism is additive, multiplicative, or event-based.

Interview-ready summary

Do not say “everything is normal.” Say: first I inspect the support, skew, tail behavior, outliers, and the data-generating process. Then I shortlist a reasonable family and validate it against plots and summary statistics.

Inference in action

Hypothesis testing

1. State the null hypothesis H0

The default assumption, usually that there is no effect or no difference.

2. State the alternative hypothesis H1

What you are trying to show, usually that an effect or difference exists.

3. Compute the test statistic

A number that measures how far your sample result is from what H0 predicts.

4. Compute the p-value

The probability of seeing a result at least this extreme if H0 is true.

5. Decide

If the p-value is below the significance level, reject H0. Otherwise, do not reject H0.

What a p-value is

The probability of observing data at least as extreme as yours, assuming H0 is true. A small p-value means the data is unlikely under H0.

What a p-value is NOT

  • It is NOT the probability that H0 is true.
  • It is NOT the probability that the result happened by chance.
  • A small p-value does not prove practical significance. Effect size matters.

Interview-ready summary

Descriptive statistics tells you the shape of what you have. Inferential statistics tells you what you can reasonably conclude beyond the sample. If you confuse the two, interviewers notice immediately.

Interview readiness

Questionnaire and scenarios

Question

When would you use the median instead of the mean?

Strong answer

When the data is skewed or has significant outliers. The median is robust to extreme values while the mean is pulled toward them.

Question

What does a p-value of 0.03 actually mean?

Strong answer

It means there is a 3% probability of observing data at least this extreme if the null hypothesis is true. It does not mean there is a 3% chance the null is true.

Scenario

Your A/B test shows a statistically significant result with a tiny effect size. Should you ship the change?

Expected reasoning

Statistical significance alone is not enough. I would check the practical effect size, business impact, and whether the improvement matters at the scale we operate at.

Scenario

You have 15 data points and want to estimate the population mean. Which distribution should you use for inference?

Expected reasoning

With a small sample size, I would use the t-distribution instead of the normal distribution because it accounts for the extra uncertainty from estimating the population standard deviation.

Scenario

Your outlet-level sales data has a median of 18000, a mean of 42000, and a few stores are far larger than the rest. Would you model it as normal?

Expected reasoning

No. The gap between mean and median plus a few dominant observations suggests strong right skew and heavy tails. I would inspect the histogram, percentiles, and maybe log transform the data. Log-normal or Pareto-like behavior is more plausible than a normal model.

Scenario

You are modeling number of customer complaints per day. The mean is 4.2 but the variance is 17.8. What does that suggest?

Expected reasoning

That count data is over-dispersed relative to Poisson, because Poisson expects mean and variance to be similar. I would consider a Negative Binomial model and also check whether excess zeros suggest a zero-inflated version.
