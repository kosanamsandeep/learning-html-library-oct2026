Optimization fundamentals

What stochastic gradient descent means

Gradient descent updates model parameters in the direction that reduces loss. Stochastic gradient descent uses only a random sample or a small batch of data to estimate that direction instead of using the full dataset every single update.

The word stochastic means there is randomness in the gradient estimate because the update is based on sampled data rather than the exact full-dataset gradient.

Core update

new weight = old weight - learning rate * gradient

Why optimization is needed

After backpropagation computes gradients, the model still needs a rule for turning those gradients into parameter updates. SGD is one of the oldest and most important such rules.

What SGD is really doing

It approximates the downhill direction on the loss surface. The estimate is noisy, but it is much cheaper than computing the exact full gradient every time on large datasets.

Why use stochastic updates

  • Faster updates on large datasets.
  • Lower memory requirements.
  • The noise can help optimization avoid some poor sharp local behavior.

What the noise means

Because each update sees only part of the data, the gradient is not exact. That makes the training path wobble more, but the method is much more practical at scale.

Method What gradient uses Main tradeoff
Batch gradient descent Full dataset Accurate but expensive and slow per update.
Stochastic gradient descent One example at a time Very noisy but updates are extremely cheap.
Mini-batch gradient descent Small batch of examples Most common practical compromise between stability and speed.

Practical note: when people casually say SGD in deep learning, they often mean mini-batch training rather than literally one sample per update.

Simple training loop

for each mini-batch:
    predictions = model(batch)
    loss = loss_fn(predictions, targets)
    gradients = backprop(loss)
    weights = weights - learning_rate * gradients

Role of learning rate

The learning rate controls step size. If it is too small, training is slow. If it is too large, updates may overshoot and training can diverge.

Why SGD is often extended

Plain SGD is foundational, but practical systems often use momentum, Adam, RMSProp, or schedulers to make optimization faster and more stable.

1. Start with a reasonable batch size

Too small can be very noisy. Too large can be memory-heavy and may generalize differently.

2. Tune the learning rate first

Learning-rate choice is usually one of the most sensitive optimization decisions.

3. Watch loss curves

If loss jumps wildly, the learning rate may be too high or gradients may be unstable.

Interview readiness

Questionnaire and scenarios

Question

Why is it called stochastic gradient descent?

Strong answer

Because it updates parameters using a randomly sampled example or mini-batch, so the computed gradient is a noisy estimate of the true full-dataset gradient.

Question

What is the difference between backpropagation and SGD?

Strong answer

Backpropagation computes gradients. SGD uses those gradients to update parameters.

Scenario

Your loss oscillates heavily and does not settle. What do you inspect first?

Expected reasoning

I would inspect the learning rate, batch size, gradient scale, and whether momentum or normalization settings are making updates unstable.

Scenario

Why do people usually use mini-batches instead of a full batch every step?

Expected reasoning

Because mini-batches are much cheaper, fit hardware better, and often give a good balance of optimization speed and training stability.

Interviews often use SGD as the entry point to test whether you understand the difference between gradients and optimization.
