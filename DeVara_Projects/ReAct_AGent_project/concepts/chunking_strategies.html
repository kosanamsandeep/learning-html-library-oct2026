Chunking strategies — pros, cons, examples, and why we need overlap

📖 Story mode — Detective Rao and the 500 evidence cards

The scene. Detective Rao gets a 10-page witness statement. She can't pin the whole booklet to the evidence board — too big, nobody can scan it. So she sits at her desk with scissors and turns it into 500 small index cards, drops each card into its own labelled drawer slot, and pins a tiny QR code on each so she can find it later.

One card = one drawer slot = one vector in the DB. 500 cards → 500 vectors. Never one giant vector for the whole booklet — she'd never find anything.

Now, how she cuts the pages decides how useful those cards are:

  • ✂️ Scissors every 100 words (fixed-size): fast, but she sometimes cuts a sentence in half — “…the suspect was last seen near the” | “old church on Tuesday.” Two useless cards.
  • 📏 Cut at paragraph → sentence → word, in that order (recursive): she tries the cleanest cut first; only falls back to messier cuts if a paragraph is too long. Almost always what you want for prose.
  • 🧠 Cut where the topic shifts (semantic): she reads first, cuts where the witness changes subject (“…and that's when he left the building. Earlier that morning…”). Slower, but each card is one pure idea.
  • 🧱 Cut along the document's own seams (structure-aware): for a contract or a PDF with headings/tables, she respects the bold headings and never splits a table row.

Why she paperclips the last line of card N onto card N+1 — chunk overlap

Sometimes a clue lives across the cut. Witness says:

...he wore a red jacket
[CUT HERE]
and carried a knife in his left pocket.

Without overlap, card 4 says “…he wore a red jacket” and card 5 says “and carried a knife…” — when Rao searches for “red jacket with a knife”, neither card matches well. With a 20-word overlap, the last line of card 4 is also stapled to the top of card 5, so card 5 now reads “…he wore a red jacket and carried a knife in his left pocket.” ✅ Match.

One-line recall trick: “500 cards in 500 drawers, paperclipped at the edges.” If you can picture the desk, scissors, drawers, and the paperclip — you've got chunking, indexing, and overlap.

Why chunk at all?

Embedding models have a max input (~512–8k tokens). Even when they don't, large chunks dilute the semantic signal — the embedding for a 5,000-word chapter encodes everything and nothing. We split documents so that each vector represents a single coherent idea and the retriever can pick the right one.

Code: 5 splitters in 10 lines

from langchain_text_splitters import (
    CharacterTextSplitter,           # fixed
    RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter,  # recursive (default!)
    TokenTextSplitter,               # sliding token
    MarkdownHeaderTextSplitter,      # structure-aware
)
splitter = RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter(
    chunk_size=1000,
    chunk_overlap=200,
    separators=["\n\n", "\n", ". ", " ", ""],
)
chunks = splitter.split_text(long_text)

Why do we need chunk_overlap?

Imagine a chunk boundary lands in the middle of an answer:

flowchart LR subgraph "No overlap" A1[...The CEO is]:::cut A2[Sarah Lee. She joined in 2020...]:::cut end subgraph "Overlap=50 tokens" B1[...The CEO is Sarah Lee. She joined...]:::ok B2[...is Sarah Lee. She joined in 2020...]:::ok end classDef cut fill:#fee2e2,stroke:#b91c1c; classDef ok fill:#dcfce7,stroke:#15803d;
Without overlapWith overlap (≈10–20% of chunk size)
"Who is the CEO?" retrieves chunk A1, which doesn't contain the name → wrong answer.Both B1 and B2 contain the full fact → either chunk is sufficient → correct answer.
Bullet lists get cut at item 4 of 8.Overlap re-includes the previous items.
Anaphora ("she", "this") loses its referent.The referent appears in both chunks.

Evaluation: how do you know your chunking is good?

SignalWhat it tells you
Retrieval recall@k (e.g. RAGAS)Are the gold chunks coming back in the top-k?
Answer faithfulnessDoes the LLM's answer cite chunks that actually support it?
Chunk length distributionLong tails → you've under-split; flat short → over-split
"Lost in the middle" testsIf recall drops for facts near chunk boundaries, increase overlap
Start with: Recursive splitter, 1000 chars, 200 overlap. Measure. Then upgrade to semantic or parent-child only if recall is poor.

Hierarchical (parent-child) variation

If you want both precise retrieval AND broad context, store both small-chunk vectors and parent-chunk vectors:

flowchart LR Page[Page 3
~2500 tokens] --> Sum[Summary chunk
1 vector] Page --> Small[50 small chunks
50 vectors] Sum --> DB[(Vector DB
now 510 rows for 10 pages)] Small --> DB DB --> Q[Query hits small chunk
→ return parent page text to LLM]

So a 10-page doc with parent-child becomes ~510 vectors (500 small + 10 page summaries) — still many separate vectors, never one giant one.

Rule: 1 chunk = 1 vector = 1 row in the DB. Always. The whole point of chunking is to get many small, precise vectors that the retriever can rank individually.
